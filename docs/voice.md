# writing voice for the website

these are the rules for all site copy in `content/walkthrough-copy.md`. they came out of a review of the redesign in october 2026, and were refined from mark's own edits to the safer-peers walkthrough, which is the reference example.

## who we're writing for

my parents, curious non-scientists, and scientists outside my field. if it's written well for them, people in my field can follow it too. an expert should never find something wrong or missing. they should just find it easy to read.

## be specific, then explain

when we point to something specific, name it the way an expert would, then say in plain words where it is or what it does. do this the first time it shows up on a page. after that, just use the name.

- regions: name the region and say what it does, not where it is: "vmPFC, a brain region that helps track how much we value different options." the 3D brains show where things are, so no anatomical location descriptions ("just behind the forehead", "on the inner surface of the temporal lobe") anywhere on the site.
- methods: "fMRI, which tracks changes in blood flow as a stand-in for brain activity."
- people and numbers: "78 teens, ages 15 to 17." keep exact sample sizes.

## a few teaching items per study

each walkthrough picks two or three key ideas that carry its main findings, names them, and defines them briefly in place, without getting too technical. name a model or parameter only where the walkthrough actually shows it.

- example: "we used a computational model, a series of equations with parameters that are fit to the choices of each participant… (the ‘social value’ parameter, called other-conferred utility in the paper)."
- introduce a named term in single quotes the first time: ‘risk preference’, ‘other-conferred utility’.

## say it plainly and precisely

- state the finding literally. no clever closing lines or aphorisms ("calling a teen ‘easily influenced’ misses…"). write "in other words, differences between substance-use groups seemed specific to safe influence."
- use accurate scientific wording: "was associated with", not "lined up with"; "often", not "almost always"; "frames", not "treats".
- lead with the finding. the subtitle should say what we found, not hint at it.

## connect the pieces

- use connectives that make the logic visible: "on the other hand…", "again, this mirrors our computational model findings…", "in other words…".
- tie brain results back to behavior explicitly.
- explain why we did something ("we estimated that boost separately for safe and risky peer choices to see if teens responded differently to each type of influence"), instead of adding method caveats.

## point to what's on screen

- refer to what the reader just did and where to look: "choices like the ones you just made", "in the plot on the right".
- each plot gets a title that names the condition shown (e.g. "safe influence" / "risky influence").

## statistics

- keep P values and test statistics out of the story text.
- put them in a clearly marked aside on the plot ("for the aficionados: the difference between the groups from the paper: P = 0.021") or in the details tab.
- only describe what's visible (e.g. mention standard-error bars only where they actually show).

## ownership

- "we" for what our team did, found, or argued: "how did we quantify extra value?", not "how do you measure…". "i" for my own take.
- for commentaries, credit the original authors for their experiment, then say clearly what we added.
- personal asides are welcome when they step outside the paper's main result, to connect studies or speculate about where it leads ("what i find most interesting…", "what excites me…"). the closing note is the natural spot.

## page hygiene

- no small label rows above games or plots that the text already introduces.
- never show the same sentence twice on a page (e.g. a caption and a readout saying the same thing).
- add a heading only when it helps someone find their place. no numbered section labels, two-beat fragments, or slogan headings.

## build the argument

- the closing note has to be earned: the steps before it should lay out each piece of evidence the conclusion rests on.
- when a conclusion is my interpretation, say so and give the reason: "that last part is why i think the brain organizes options as a map: …"
- explain design choices a curious reader would wonder about ("in the lab, we turned them into amounts of money and chances of winning, which we can control precisely").
- situate findings in what's already known ("the two brain regions we expected… where grid-like signals have been found before").
- point to the main result explicitly when it helps: "importantly, and what we found most interesting, …"
- use confident verbs when the evidence supports them ("this study shows…"), never stronger than the paper.

## examples

- pick one concrete, relatable example per walkthrough (the café with a menu and reviews) and carry it from the short version through the steps.
- give brain regions their full role, not a one-word label (medial prefrontal cortex both represents maps and tracks value).

## energy

- a little enthusiasm is welcome where it's earned: an exclamation point at a discovery or an invitation ("…see what *your* map looks like!"), a playful aside ("this is not an accident! … but i digress…"). about one per section, not more.
- invite the reader in page intros ("check out my interactive walkthroughs or dive straight into the papers").
- italics (`*word*`) for a single contrasting word: *relative*, *subjective*, *your*.

## titles

- titles state the finding in the third person ("when peer pressure makes teens safer", "preferences shape how the brain maps options"). no "you/your" in titles; second person belongs inside the walkthrough, where the reader is actually playing.

## interface

- fewer controls: show only what the current step needs (strategy buttons live in the details tab, not the story).
- no captions that repeat the text, no sharing links, no label rows.
- put the result on the visual itself instead of in a separate panel or dropdown.
- no instruction hints ("select a dot to see…") or default readouts that restate the plot (group means ± standard error); a readout appears only for something the reader picked.
- games move to the next round on their own, like the peer and fog games, and keep their results while the reader moves around the page.
- the 3D brain is the description of where a region is: no separate location or anatomy notes.

## disclaimers

- keep "preprint" (not yet peer reviewed).
- no label rows above plots ("an illustration", "44 people · study data"); an imaginary example can say so in its caption.
- no association-vs-causation boilerplate; show it in the wording ("was associated with").

## words and style

- lowercase, except proper nouns, names, and acronyms (vmPFC, fMRI, UCLA, Raithel).
- "participants", not "subjects". "teens" for the adolescent study.
- hyphenate compound modifiers: "risk-taking behavior", "substance-use groups".
- curly quotes and apostrophes (’ ‘ ’ “ ”).
- tone: conversational but a notch more formal than casual. longer sentences and parentheticals are fine. a little personality where it fits ("for the aficionados").
- avoid: "the key is", "what's more", "not X but Y" setups, rhetorical-question openers, piles of em dashes, "something to go on".
- never make a finding sound stronger than the paper does.
