### introduction and methods

### gist-title

following can be a shortcut.

### gist-1

<!-- copy: social-conformity.page.gist-1 -->
when a decision is unfamiliar, other people’s choices can give us something to go on. we studied this possibility using a gambling task and participants who already had lesions in brain regions involved in evaluating risk. compared with healthy adults, they relied more on a shortcut of following others’ choices.
<!-- /copy -->

### gist-2

<!-- copy: social-conformity.page.gist-2 -->
many healthy adults used this shortcut too. across participants, greater difficulty explaining solo choices from their own risk preferences went with more reliance on following. the broader question is when people lean on others because evaluating the options themselves is difficult.
<!-- /copy -->

### gist-caveat

<!-- copy: social-conformity.page.gist-caveat -->
participants with lesions were recruited from a hospital registry after prior surgery; the lesions were not created for this experiment.
<!-- /copy -->

### deep-title

<!-- copy: social-conformity.page.deep-title -->
adding value, following, or opposing.
<!-- /copy -->

### deep-1

<!-- copy: social-conformity.page.deep-1 -->
the hybrid model allows three contributions. people can evaluate the gambles using their own risk preferences, with peers’ choices adding value to an option (other-conferred utility). they can also follow or oppose others without that value calculation. fitting those ingredients together helps distinguish “their choice makes this worth more to me” from “i’ll do what they did.”
<!-- /copy -->

### deep-2

<!-- copy: social-conformity.page.deep-2 -->
the group difference was not just more following. across the combined lesion group, the model also assigned less weight to value-based processing; the evidence in the separate-region comparisons was clearest for insula lesions. the tendency to oppose others did not show a detected group difference. those results help specify which contributions changed, rather than treating all social influence as the same process.
<!-- /copy -->

### deep-method

<!-- copy: social-conformity.page.deep-method -->
statistics are reported from the paper. solo mismatch is minus the median log likelihood across posterior draws, following the published analysis code.
<!-- /copy -->

### deep-limitations


### takeaway-caveat

<!-- copy: social-conformity.page.takeaway-caveat -->
the unfamiliar-decisions connection is a broader interpretation; it was not directly tested here.
<!-- /copy -->


### takeaway

even though we used lesion groups, many healthy adults still used this mechanism to some extent. i think that’s especially relevant when we make decisions in areas we don’t know much about: other people’s choices may give us something to go on. that broader possibility is what excites me about this work.
